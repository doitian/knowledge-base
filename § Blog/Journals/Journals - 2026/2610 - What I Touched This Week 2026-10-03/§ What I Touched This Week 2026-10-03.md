## Don't Get Addicted to Painkillers

Dinesh Raju's [Don't Get Addicted to Painkillers](https://dinesh.eth.link/notes/painkillers/) is about the relief that makes a bad model stick. Not knowing what's going on is exhausting. The mind wants the competing possibilities closed. A borrowed explanation does that, and the tiredness goes away.

- A painkiller makes everything click. A useful explanation keeps the messy, context-specific details and the uncontrollable bits that can make the plan fail. Borrowing is only the first step; the painkiller is raw material for a version of the model that works for you.
- Manuel Bärenz's [How to Keep Enjoying Programming in a World of LLMs](https://discourse.haskell.org/t/how-to-keep-enjoying-programming-in-a-world-of-llms/14705) points the same rule at agents. Don't let it make crucial decisions; make it ask you. If you don't understand the question, it failed to hand you the context—or you need a break. Research in parallel so you roughly know what the agent knows. Plan together, then you code.
- Alex Kladov's [Finding Bugs](https://matklad.github.io/2026/09/19/finding-bugs.html) is the testing form of not borrowing the answer. Compare against an **oracle**: implement the fast version and a known-right slow one, and match them. Fuzzing against an oracle is effective, which is a reason to build the oracle. Prefer small, tricky examples over large uniform ones.

The same week, in practice: I used plannotator a lot. [GitNexus](https://github.com/abhigyanpatwari/GitNexus) ran in the background, and I still can't tell whether it helped. [You Said No MCP!](https://earendil.com/posts/you-said-no-mcp/) names the related constraint. MCP is still hard to compose. The useful move is a codemode-style call that combines tools and returns the answer without spending the context window.

## The Senior Engineer Death Spiral

Sunil Pai's [The Senior Engineer Death Spiral](https://sunilpai.dev/posts/the-senior-engineer-death-spiral/) is what happens when the job becomes performing seniority instead of moving the work.

- The fix is to drop a level. Not to try to be a higher-level engineer, but to become the greatest teammate for a while.
- Shift from an outcome mindset to a momentum mindset. Build a routine. Momentum is the thing.
- Projects finish by slow, steady, incremental work—daily, hourly, until it's muscle memory—not by a weekly burst of importance.

Sujith Jay Nair's [A Staff Engineer's Guide to Inventing Work](https://sujithjay.com/inventing-work) is the other half, once the job is figuring out what a platform team should build next. A cheap sensor: if a manager, or their manager, mentions something twice in a week, there is likely an unaddressed concern behind it. Next to Pai, that sensor only works up close.

## One Shortcut, Two Voice Inputs

I worked out a dictation chord with two destinations. [hyprwhspr](https://github.com/doitian/hyprwhspr) is system-wide speech-to-text. Claude already has its own voice input. The point is to keep the muscle memory and switch the target.

- RAlt+Space fires global dictation. RAlt+Shift+Space toggles that same key over to Claude's local voice input. The [keyd layers](https://gist.github.com/doitian/02a381065076d3a9ad31154607872915) need an explicit three-way binding so the toggle still wins while the mode is on—otherwise the two composite layers fight over Space. I wrote the config; applying it needs sudo, so it is not on the machine yet.
- The other direction is listening. I made a voice clone for [qwen-tts-reader](https://github.com/doitian/qwen-tts-reader), a terminal article reader with streaming speech, synced text, and resume.
